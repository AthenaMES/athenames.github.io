Ask a planner how a heat treatment or plating vendor is doing, and the answer is usually a feeling. "He is fine, mostly." "He was late last week." "He is always slow before Diwali." The feeling may be right, but nobody can put a number on it.

Without numbers, two things go wrong. The plan keeps using a lead time that does not match reality, and the monthly talk with the vendor turns into an argument about memories instead of a review of facts.

Measuring vendor lead time and on-time delivery fixes both. You need only a few dates for every lot you send out, and one simple calculation each month. This article explains what to measure, how to measure it fairly, and how to use the result.

## Two numbers that tell you most of the story

For each vendor, and each type of work they do for you, track two things:

- **Vendor lead time:** how many days a lot takes from the day it leaves your gate to the day it comes back. This tells the planner how many days to allow for the vendor stage.
- **On-time delivery:** out of all lots due back in a period, how many came back on or before the due date. This tells you how far you can trust the vendor's promise.

They answer different questions. A vendor can be slow but reliable: always 8 days, never late against an 8-day due date. Another can be fast on average but unpredictable. The planner needs both numbers to plan well.

A third check is useful too: **in full**. Did the whole quantity come back, or only part of it? A lot that arrives on time but 10 pieces short still stops the next stage. Many companies combine the two checks into one measure called "on-time, in-full". Definitions vary from company to company, as [McKinsey points out](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/operations/our-insights/defining-on-time-in-full-in-the-consumer-sector), so write down your own definition and use it the same way every month.

## Agree the rules before you measure

A number is only fair if both sides understand it. Settle these points first:

1. **One due date per lot.** Every lot sent out gets a written due date when it leaves the gate. "Next week" is not a due date.
2. **Which date counts as "back".** The simplest rule is the date the lot is received at your gate. Use the same rule for every vendor.
3. **What "on time" means.** For example: received on or before the due date. Decide whether one day late counts as late. A strict, simple rule is the easiest to apply.
4. **What "in full" means.** For example: the full quantity sent is received, counting good and rejected pieces together. Rejections are a quality question; track them separately so you do not mix two problems.
5. **Lead time by type of work.** Case hardening and zinc plating at the same vendor are different jobs. Measure them separately.

## How to measure vendor lead time and on-time delivery: step by step

1. **Record every lot on one line.** Job card number, vendor, type of work, quantity sent, date sent, due date, date received and quantity received. Our article on [tracking material at subcontractors]({{ '/blog/track-material-subcontractors-challans-not-enough/' | relative_url }}) shows a simple send and receive record you can use.
2. **Fill the receive date the same day.** If the stores team writes receipts at the end of the week, the dates become guesses. Ask them to write it when the vehicle arrives.
3. **Keep partial returns open.** If 45 of 50 pieces come back, record 45 received and leave 5 open against the vendor until they arrive or are written off.
4. **Calculate lead time for each lot.** Days from date sent to date received.
5. **Mark each lot on time or late, in full or short.** Use the rules you agreed.
6. **Count at month-end.** For each vendor and type of work, note the number of lots, the average lead time, the slowest lot, the share of lots on time and the share in full.
7. **Look at the late lots, not only the totals.** Were they small lots? Were they sent on a Friday? Did they wait at your gate for a vehicle? Some causes sit on your side.
8. **Share the sheet with the vendor.** A plain, dated record is easier to discuss than a complaint. Agree one or two actions and check them next month.
9. **Feed the lead time back into the plan.** If the real lead time is longer than the plan, change the plan. Our article on [planning vendor stages around real lead times]({{ '/blog/plan-vendor-stages-real-lead-times/' | relative_url }}) explains how.

## A worked example

For example, take a sample machine shop (not a real customer) that sends parts to one plating vendor. Each lot gets a due date 6 days after it is sent. This is sample data, shown only to explain the method.

| Lot | Qty sent | Sent | Due back | Received | Qty received | Days | On time | In full |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 100 | 3 Nov | 9 Nov | 8 Nov | 100 | 5 | Yes | Yes |
| 2 | 60 | 5 Nov | 11 Nov | 12 Nov | 60 | 7 | No | Yes |
| 3 | 80 | 7 Nov | 13 Nov | 13 Nov | 72 | 6 | Yes | No |
| 4 | 120 | 10 Nov | 16 Nov | 15 Nov | 120 | 5 | Yes | Yes |
| 5 | 40 | 12 Nov | 18 Nov | 21 Nov | 40 | 9 | No | Yes |
| 6 | 90 | 14 Nov | 20 Nov | 19 Nov | 90 | 5 | Yes | Yes |
| 7 | 70 | 17 Nov | 23 Nov | 23 Nov | 70 | 6 | Yes | Yes |
| 8 | 50 | 19 Nov | 25 Nov | 28 Nov | 45 | 9 | No | No |

The month-end count for this sample vendor:

- **Lots:** 8
- **Average lead time:** 52 days in total across 8 lots, so 6.5 days
- **Slowest lots:** 9 days (lots 5 and 8)
- **On time:** 5 of 8 lots (62.5%)
- **In full:** 6 of 8 lots (75%)
- **On time and in full:** 4 of 8 lots (50%)

What the planner learns:

1. **The 6-day due date is too tight.** The average is above 6 days, and the slow lots take 9. Planning 6 days for this stage will keep causing late jobs.
2. **The slowest lots were the two smallest.** Lots 5 and 8 had 40 and 50 pieces. They may have waited for a full plating batch. The planner asks the vendor before deciding.
3. **Short quantities need follow-up.** Lot 3 is 8 pieces short and lot 8 is 5 pieces short. Both balances stay open against the vendor.

The planner agrees two actions with the vendor: combine small lots where possible, and send a message when a lot will miss its due date. Next month, the same table shows whether they worked.

## Common mistakes

- **Measuring only the late lots.** You need every lot, or the percentage means nothing.
- **Changing the due date after the fact.** If the due date moves, record the new date separately. Keep the original for measurement.
- **Mixing types of work.** A fast plating job and a slow nitriding job averaged together hide both problems.
- **Treating one bad month as the truth.** Look at two or three months before you change vendors.
