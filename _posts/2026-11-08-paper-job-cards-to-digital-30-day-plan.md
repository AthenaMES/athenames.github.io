Most machine shops do not run on software. They run on paper job cards, a production register, an Excel sheet in the PPC office and a lot of WhatsApp messages. That system has carried many factories a long way. But there is a point where it stops keeping up: when the number of parts, machines, vendors and customers grows faster than the people who keep the registers.

If your supervisors spend the morning asking "where is this job?", or month-end figures take a week to reconcile, you are at that point. The good news is that moving from **paper job cards to digital** records does not need sensors, new machines or a big-bang project. It needs a clear plan and a small start.

Here is a practical 30-day plan that a growing machine shop can follow.

## Why paper job cards stop working

Paper is not the problem. Paper is honest: the operator writes what happened. The trouble starts when that information has to travel.

- **It arrives late.** A job card reaches the office at the end of a shift, or the end of the job. By then the plan for the day is already wrong.
- **It splits and gets lost.** When 40 of 60 pieces move to the next stage and 20 stay behind, one card becomes two stories. Often only one gets written down.
- **It cannot answer questions.** "Which lots went to the heat treatment vendor last Tuesday?" means searching through files.
- **It depends on memory.** When the one person who knows the status is on leave, the plant slows down.

None of this is anyone's fault. It is simply more information than paper can move quickly.

## The 30-day plan

### Week 1: Map what really happens

Before you change anything, walk the floor with the people who run it.

1. Pick **one product family**, for example shafts or gears, not the whole plant.
2. List every stage it passes through, in order: cutting, turning, hobbing, heat treatment, grinding, inspection, dispatch.
3. Mark which stages are done **in-house** and which go to a **vendor**.
4. Write down the exceptions you see every week: split quantities, rework, alternate machines, vendor delays.
5. Collect one real paper job card and note every field the team actually fills in.

This map is the most valuable document you will make. Software should follow your process, not the other way round.

### Week 2: Decide what to record, and who records it

Keep the first version small. For each stage, decide the few facts that matter:

| What to record | Who records it | When |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Quantity started and completed | Operator | At the end of each job or shift |
| Stops and the reason | Operator | When the stop happens |
| Tool used | Operator | At the start of the job |
| Rejected or rework quantity | Inspector | At inspection |
| Sent to vendor and received back | Stores or PPC | At send and receive |

Write stop reasons and rejection reasons as a short list in the words your operators already use. If your team speaks Hindi, Tamil, Bengali or Swahili on the floor, the reasons should be in that language too.

### Week 3: Start in one zone

Choose one zone, such as the CNC cell that feeds your bottleneck. Put one computer in that production area. In many plants, one shared computer is enough for the operators of about four machines.

1. Train the operators and the supervisor of that zone only, at the machine, in short sessions.
2. Run paper and digital side by side for the first week.
3. Every evening, the supervisor compares the two. Where they differ, fix the reason, not the person.

Running both for a short time builds trust. Operators see that the screen shows exactly what they entered.

### Week 4: Use the data, then expand

Now the records start paying back.

- Hold a **15-minute review** each morning using the digital records instead of phone calls.
- Look for the stage where work **waits** the longest. In many shops it is a vendor stage or a shared machine.
- Check that split lots and rework appear as their own steps, so no pieces go missing.

When the first zone runs smoothly for a week, add the next zone. Expanding zone by zone keeps the change at a pace your team can handle.

## An example

For example, take a job card for 60 pinion shafts. On paper, the card says "Hobbing: 60" and moves on. In reality, 40 pieces finished hobbing on Monday and went to heat treatment, while 20 stayed behind because of a tool change.

With digital records, that becomes two lots: 40 pieces at the vendor and 20 pieces still at hobbing, each with its own status and date. When the customer calls about the order, the planner can answer in seconds instead of walking the floor.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Starting with the whole plant.** One zone first. Always.
- **Recording too much.** If an entry takes more than a minute, operators will skip it.
- **Buying hardware first.** You usually do not need sensors or machine connections to start. Operator entries on a shared computer are enough.
- **Leaving supervisors out.** If supervisors do not use the records in their daily review, operators will stop entering them.
