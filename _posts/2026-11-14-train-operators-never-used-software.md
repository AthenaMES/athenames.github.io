Many good operators have never used a computer at work. They can read a drawing, set a tool offset and hear when an insert is going, but a mouse, a login screen or a drop-down list is new to them. When a factory moves from paper to a screen, the question is not whether they are capable. It is how to **train operators who have never used software** without slowing down production or making anyone feel small.

The usual approach does not work well. A trainer takes twenty people into a meeting room, shows a projector for two hours, and hands out a printed manual. Back at the machine the next day, most people remember very little. Entries are skipped, or the supervisor quietly does them all at the end of the shift.

This article gives a simple method that fits a busy shop floor: short sessions, at the actual computer, on real job cards, one task at a time.

## Why classroom training fails on the shop floor

Operators learn their trade by watching, trying and being corrected at the machine. Software training should follow the same pattern. A classroom has three problems:

- **It is far from the work.** The screen in the meeting room is not the screen next to lathe 3.
- **It covers too much.** Logins, job cards, stops, rejections, tools and reports in one session is more than anyone can hold.
- **It uses sample data that feels unreal.** People remember tasks they did on their own job cards, not on "Part ABC".

There is also fear. An operator who has never used software may worry about pressing the wrong key, losing data, or looking slow in front of younger colleagues. Good training removes that fear first.

## A proven pattern: show, try, check

Manufacturers have trained new workers with a simple four-step method for decades. It comes from the Training Within Industry (TWI) programme, whose Job Instruction method is still taught today ([Lean Enterprise Institute: Training Within Industry](https://www.lean.org/lexicon-terms/training-within-industry-twi/)). In short:

1. **Prepare the person:** put them at ease and explain why the task matters.
2. **Present the task:** show it, one step at a time, and explain the key points.
3. **Let them try:** they do it while you watch and correct gently.
4. **Follow up:** check back often at first, then less as they become confident.

The same steps work very well for software. The rest of this article applies them to shop floor data entry.

## How to train operators, step by step

### 1. Start with the "why", in their language

Before anyone touches the keyboard, explain in two or three sentences why entries matter. For example: "When you record a stop, the supervisor sees it straight away and can arrange inserts or material before the next job waits." Use the language the team speaks on the floor. If the screen labels can be in that language too, show that first. It removes a lot of worry.

### 2. Train at the real computer, in small groups

Train two or three operators at a time, at the computer in their own production area, during a quiet part of the shift. Twenty to thirty minutes is enough for one session. If you have not yet set up area computers, our guide on [one computer per production area]({{ '/blog/one-computer-per-production-area-setup/' | relative_url }}) explains where to place them.

### 3. Teach one task per session

Do not teach the whole system at once. A sensible order for operators:

1. Log in and log out with their own name.
2. Start a job card.
3. Record a stop and pick the reason.
4. Record the quantity completed.
5. Record a tool change.

Inspectors and supervisors need different tasks (rejections, rework, splits, sending material to a vendor). Train each role only on what that role records. Our [job card checklist]({{ '/blog/what-job-card-should-record-every-stage/' | relative_url }}) shows which fields belong at each stage.

### 4. Use their own job cards

Train on a job that is actually running on their machine today. The operator already knows the part, the quantity and the stage, so the only new thing is the screen. This also means the first entries are real data, not practice.

### 5. Let them press every key

The trainer should not hold the mouse. Show the step once, then hand over. Let the operator make mistakes while you are standing beside them, and show how to correct a wrong entry. Knowing that a mistake can be fixed is what makes people willing to try.

### 6. Leave a one-page picture guide

Next to each computer, stick a single page with screenshots of the three or four most common entries, with short labels in the local language. Keep it to one page. A thick manual will not be opened.

### 7. Name a helper in each area

In every area, pick one operator who learns quickly and is respected by the others. Give them a little extra training. They become the first person others ask, which is easier than calling the supervisor or the office.

### 8. Follow up daily for the first two weeks

For the first days, the supervisor or helper checks entries during the shift, not at the end. Praise correct entries in front of the team. When an entry is missing, ask about it the same day and help the operator do it. After two weeks, move to a weekly check.

## A worked example

For example, take a sample turning area (not a real plant) with four CNC lathes and five operators, two of whom have never used a computer. A training plan could look like this:

| Day | Session (25 minutes) | Who | Practised on |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Day 1 | Why entries matter; log in and log out | All five | Their own login |
| Day 2 | Start a job card | Two operators per session | Job card JC-4021, 80 flanges |
| Day 3 | Record a stop and its reason | Two operators per session | A real stop from that shift |
| Day 4 | Record quantity completed | Two operators per session | JC-4021, first 40 pieces |
| Day 5 | Record a tool change; recap | All five, with the area helper | Live jobs |

On day 3, one of the new operators records a 15-minute stop as "waiting for material" instead of "waiting for setter". The trainer shows him how to correct it, and he does it himself. From week 2, the area helper checks entries twice a shift and the supervisor reviews missing entries once a day.

Reports and settings are not in the plan. Operators do not need them.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Training everyone in one long session.** Short and frequent beats long and rare.
- **Training on dummy data.** Use real job cards from day one.
- **Training operators on supervisor tasks.** It confuses people and adds nothing.
- **Changing the screen after training.** If fields or reasons change, retrain that one task at the computer.
- **Blaming people for missed entries.** Ask why. Often the reason list is missing an option or the computer is too far away.
