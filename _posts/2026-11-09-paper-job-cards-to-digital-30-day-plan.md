Are you still running the shop floor with only paper job cards, registers and spreadsheets? Let’s walk through a simple 30-day plan to move it to digital records, one stage at a time, without IoT or new hardware.

As per UNIDO, more than 50% of surveyed companies with annual turnover below ₹75 crore had no ERP system which means machine shops do not run on any kind of digital systems and they run on a physical production register, an excel or spreadsheets and a lot of WhatsApp messages. That system has carried many factories a long way. At the initial stage it's feasible but there is a point where it stops keeping up: when the number of parts or assemblies, machines, vendors, processes gets complicated and customers grow faster than the people who keep the registers.

If you, your production, planning, dispatch team and supervisors spend their morning asking "where is this job?", or month-end figures take a week to reconcile, you are at that point. The good news is that moving from **paper job cards to digital** records does not need sensors, new machines or a big bang project and huge investment. It just needs a clear plan, your will and a small start.

Here is a proven practical next 30 day plan that a growing machine shop like you can follow.

## Why paper job cards stop working

Paper is not the problem. The paper is honest: the operator writes what happened. The trouble starts when, how and where that information has to travel.

- **It arrives late.** A job card or production record reaches the office at the end of a shift, or the end of the job. By then the plan for the day had already changed due to tons of reasons.
- **If it splits then gets lost.** When 50 of 60 pieces move to the next stage and 10 stay behind, one card becomes two stories and only one carries the truth as often only one gets written down.
- **It cannot answer questions.** "Which lots went to the heat treatment vendor last Tuesday?" means finding through files and wasting time and passing another day.
- **It depends on the memory and monopoly of the highest experienced person.** When the one person who knows everything is on leave, the plant goes down.

None of this is anyone's fault. It is simply paper’s capability of moving of information at right pace and right time.

## The 30-day plan

### Week 1: Map what really happens - The As is Process

Before you think of changing anything or implementing any tools first, walk the floor with the people who run it.

1. Pick one product or assembly family, for example shafts or gears, not the whole plant.
2. List every process stage it passes through in detail, in order: cutting, turning, hobbing, heat treatment, grinding, inspection, dispatch.
3. Mark which stages are done in house and which go to an outsource.
4. Write down the actual problems working people see every week, take feedback from operator to supervisor level to get the truth and real problems: split quantities, rework, alternate machines, vendor delays; do not rely on your own instinct of this is how it should be done instead focus on taking the real steps being followed.
5. Collect one real paper job card and note every field the team actually fills in.

This map is the most valuable document you will make.

### Week 2: Decide what to record, and who records it and when it should be recorded

Keep the first version small. For each stage, decide the few facts that matter:

| What to record | Who records it | When |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Quantity completed | Operator | At the end of each job or shift |
| Stops and the reason for stop as loss | Operator | When the stop happens |
| Tool utilised and its life | Operator | At the end of the job |
| Rejected or rework | Quality inspector | At in process inspection |
| Sent for outsource and received back | Stores or PPC | At send and receive |

Write stop reasons as loss and rejection reasons as a standardised short list in the words your operators already use. If your team speaks Hindi, Tamil, Bengali or Gujarati on the shop floor, the reasons should be in that language too. Design, deploy and get feedback from real users. At initial stage, resistance will be faced but later user will only provide the inputs on improving the processes.

### Week 3: Start in one zone

Choose one zone or one major process, such as the CNC - VMC cell that seems to have a bottleneck. Put one PC system in that production area. In many plants, one shared computer is more than enough for the operators to begin with for around four to five machines.

1. Train the operators and the supervisor of that zone only, at the machine, in short sessions and carry spaced and repeated training. Take continuous feedback.
2. Run paper and digital side by side for the first week.
3. Every evening, the supervisor needs to compare the two. Where they differ, fix the reason, not the person.

Running both for a short time builds trust. Operators see that the screen shows exactly what they entered.

### Week 4: Use the data, then expand

Now the records start paying back.

- Hold a 20 minute review meeting each morning using the digital records instead of phone calls.
- Look for the stage where work waits the longest.
- Look for hidden losses, prioritise and encourage operators to resolve them through continuous improvements

When the first zone runs smoothly for a week, move to the next zone. Expanding zone by zone keeps the change at a pace your team can handle. Along with that, take the best performer of the previous zone’s person to train the other zone to encourage and remove the frictions in the implementation.

For example, take a job card for 70 pinion shafts. On paper, the card says "Hobbing: 70" and moves on. In reality, 50 pieces finished hobbing on Tuesday and went to heat treatment, while 20 stayed behind because of a high setup timings.

With digital records, that becomes two lots: 50 pieces at the vendor and 20 pieces still at hobbing station, each with its own status and date. When the customer calls about the order, the planner can answer in seconds instead of walking the floor.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Starting with the whole plant.** One zone or process first. Always.
- **Recording too much.** If an entry takes more than a minute, operators will skip it. Try to find a way to optimize it.
- **Buying hardware first.** You usually do not need sensors or machine connections to start. Operator entries on a shared computer are enough.
- **Leaving supervisors out.** If supervisors do not use the records in their day to day reviews.
